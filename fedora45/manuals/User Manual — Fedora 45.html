    Chapter 1

    What REMOTIX is

    1.1 · REMOTIX in short

    REMOTIX shows the desktop of a Linux machine inside a browser tab. You open the machine's address in the browser of a PC, a tablet or a phone, sign in with the Linux user name and password you already have on that machine, and your own desktop appears, with mouse, keyboard, clipboard and sound. There is nothing to install on the device you connect from.

    What you get:

    • Your own desktop. Every user of the machine gets a desktop of their own. Several people can work on the same machine at the same time, each in their own desktop and without seeing the others.
    • The size of your window. The desktop is created as large as the browser window at the moment you sign in. The size you really get depends on the device and the browser you connect from; the program limits the desktop to 4096×2304 pixels at most.
    • Keyboard, mouse and touch. On a PC, and on a tablet or phone with a mouse and keyboard (for example Samsung DeX), you work as at the machine. On a touch screen without a mouse the page switches by itself to a touch layout (see 3.6 “On a phone or a tablet”).
    • Sound. What the desktop plays, you hear in the browser.
    • Copy and paste. Text can be copied between your device and the remote desktop, in both directions (text only: not images or files).
    • Leave and come back. Closing the tab, a dropped line or moving to another device does not close the desktop: its programs keep running. Sign in again, from the same device or from another one, and you find your windows as you left them. It is closed, with its programs, if the service is stopped or restarted, if the machine is switched off, or if you sign in at the machine's own screen with the same user: unsaved work is then lost (see 3.5 “Leaving and coming back”).
    • Encrypted. Everything travels encrypted, on a single port, 7447.
    i
    How many people? how many people one machine can serve at once depends on its video card and on how large the windows are. The program's default ceiling is 10 sessions (the administrator can change it with --session-cap); a machine may well serve fewer. On Fedora 45 no more than three people were tried together (by the automatic tests, on GNOME), and no figure for the capacity of a machine exists yet.

    1.2 · Requirements

    On Fedora 45 REMOTIX works only if five conditions are met. The video card one (number 2) is not given by Fedora itself, so read it before you install.

    Limits that are declared, not defects. These are known and were decided, on Fedora 45:

    • A sign-in at the machine's own screen with the same user closes that user's remote desktop with its programs: unsaved work is lost (see 3.5 “Leaving and coming back”).
    • No unlocked keyring. A program that asks for the user's keyring (the stored passwords of the desktop) finds it locked in a remote session and asks for the password on the remote screen. At the machine itself the keyring is unlocked by the sign-in; REMOTIX does not do that for a remote one.
    • XFCE has a black background and no icons on the desktop. Fedora's XFCE desktop program was built without Wayland support; the panel and the dock are there (see 1.5 “The desktops”).
    • No power off, restart, suspend or hibernate from any desktop, for any user (see 1.4 “What REMOTIX is not”).
    • KDE Plasma needs KWin 6.8 or newer, which Fedora 45 has; with an older KWin REMOTIX refuses to start the Plasma desktop instead of showing a wrong one.

    1.3 · What you need

    Five things have to be in place.

    The browser has to be recent: it needs two features, WebTransport and WebCodecs, and the page tells you in one sentence when one is missing (see ch. 4 “When something does not work”). On Fedora 45 the automatic tests used browsers on Linux; by hand, the owner of the project connected from a Linux PC, from a Windows notebook and from an Android device. The other rows of the table are what the design says, not something tried on Fedora 45.

    i
    What the device needs. a computer, a tablet or a phone; no app or plug-in. The device only has to decode video, which every recent browser does. If your device decodes HEVC, REMOTIX uses it; otherwise it uses H.264. Firefox on Linux uses H.264. The clock of the device has to be right (see 1.2 “Requirements”).

    1.4 · What REMOTIX is not

    What REMOTIX deliberately does not do:

    • It does not encode video on the processor. No video card able to encode, no desktop in the browser.
    • It has no sign-in of its own. There are no REMOTIX accounts and no REMOTIX passwords: you sign in with the user name and password of the machine, checked by the machine's own authentication (PAM). The administrator account, root, cannot sign in this way.
    • It does not install a desktop, and does not let you choose one from the page: the machine's desktop is used (see 1.5 “The desktops”).
    • No file transfer, and no images or files in the clipboard: text only.
    • No microphone: sound goes from the desktop to you, not back.
    • No power off, restart, suspend or hibernate from any desktop, remote or at the machine itself, for any user: it is wanted, because other people may be working on the machine (an administrator still can, with sudo in a terminal). And no screen lock inside a remote desktop.
    • No several monitors, no session recording, no X11 desktop sessions (X11 programs run through XWayland), no connections from RDP, VNC or SPICE programs.
    • No unlocked keyring in a remote session. REMOTIX does not add the keyring modules to the sign-in: a program that asks for the keyring finds it locked and asks for the password on the remote screen. This is declared and was decided (it is the same on the other distributions REMOTIX runs on).

    1.5 · The desktops

    On Fedora 45 REMOTIX works with the four desktops of Fedora: GNOME (Fedora Workstation), KDE Plasma, XFCE and LXQt. It uses the desktop already installed on the machine.

    • One desktop per machine. REMOTIX is made for a machine that has one of the four desktops: machines with several desktops installed together are not supported. If it finds more than one, it still picks one (when the machine's login screen is set to a desktop, the choice of the login screen decides; otherwise GNOME, then KDE Plasma, then XFCE, then LXQt) and says so in the service's journal, on the line “the desktop of this machine”, but this is a line that tells the truth, not a feature.
    • The login screens are GDM on GNOME, plasmalogin (package plasma-login-manager) on KDE Plasma, LightDM on XFCE and SDDM on LXQt. They matter when somebody signs in at the machine itself (see 3.5 “Leaving and coming back”).
    • GNOME uses its own compositor (Mutter). KDE Plasma runs on KWin alone (version 6.8), with no second compositor under it. XFCE and LXQt run on a small compositor called labwc, the one of Fedora's repositories: it is a recommended package, so dnf installs it with REMOTIX unless weak dependencies are switched off (see 2.3 “Fedora 45: installing”).
    • XFCE: black background, no desktop icons. This is a declared limit on Fedora 45: XFCE's desktop program was built without Wayland support, so the remote XFCE desktop has a plain black background and no icons on it. The panel at the top and the dock at the bottom are there. Choosing another wallpaper does not help.
    • GNOME: the Overview. The remote GNOME desktop may start in the Activities Overview, and REMOTIX does not leave it by itself: press Escape or choose a program. (This was not checked again with the GNOME of Fedora 45.)
    • No desktop at all on the machine: the service says so at start-up and no desktop can be shown.
    i
    Your settings. REMOTIX applies a few rules to the desktop while a remote session lasts (the shut down, restart, suspend and lock buttons are not there, and “Log Out” is always shown) and takes them away again at the end. Everything else you change from the browser — look, themes, panels — stays as you set it, and you find it again at the next sign-in; on GNOME this needs the dconf command (package dconf, installed with REMOTIX as a recommended package). More on this in 5.4 “Users, groups and what REMOTIX changes”.

    1.6 · The licence

    REMOTIX is free for personal use and for non-profit associations, churches and religious institutes; any other use needs a written licence from the author (remotix@nicfio.it). The source code may be read and checked, but not modified or redistributed.

    The licence concerns whoever installs and runs the server: people who only connect with a browser need nothing from it. Its full text is in /usr/share/licenses/remotix/LICENSE.md after installation, and the licences of the third-party components are in /usr/share/doc/remotix/THIRD-PARTY-LICENSES. This summary is not the licence; the text is.

    Chapter 2

    Installing REMOTIX

    2.1 · The steps

    Installing REMOTIX is the installation of one package with dnf. There is no installer program. These are the steps; the commands are in the sections below. REMOTIX 1.2.0 is for Fedora 45 (Workstation with GNOME, KDE Plasma Desktop, and the Xfce and LXQt spins).

    1. Check the machine: Fedora 45 with one of the four desktops installed, a video card that encodes (see 1.3 “What you need”), and a way to reach it on the network.
    2. Enable RPM Fusion and install the video driver, before the package. Fedora does not carry the H.264 driver of the video card and REMOTIX cannot show a desktop without it (see 2.2 “Fedora 45: video drivers (RPM Fusion)”).
    3. Install the package with dnf, as an administrator.
    4. Switch the service on. The package installs the service switched off and does not start it: you decide when REMOTIX starts to listen.
    5. Open the firewall: port 7447, TCP and UDP, with firewalld. REMOTIX never changes the firewall (see 2.5 “Fedora 45: the firewall”).
    6. Open the page for the first time from a browser and accept the machine's certificate once on each device (see 2.6 “Fedora 45: the first visit and the certificate”).

    Nothing has to be configured for the people who will connect: any user of the machine, except the ones in the list of excluded users (by default only root), can sign in with the machine's own password. The first time a user connects, REMOTIX prepares what that user needs to use the video card (see 5.4 “Users, groups and what REMOTIX changes”).

    2.2 · Fedora 45: video drivers (RPM Fusion)

    REMOTIX encodes the desktop as H.264 video on the video card, and nowhere else. On Fedora the drivers that let the card do it are not in the base repositories: Fedora's own Mesa is built without H.264 and HEVC. They come from RPM Fusion, a separate repository that the administrator enables. This is a requirement of REMOTIX, like the video card itself: the package does not bring the drivers in by itself, and there is no software encoding to fall back on.

    The commands below are the set of drivers that the test machines had. Run them before installing REMOTIX, so that the test at the end of the installation passes (see 2.3 “Fedora 45: installing”). All of them need an administrator.

    1. Enable RPM Fusion (free).Enable RPM Fusion
      $ sudo dnf install https://mirrors.rpmfusion.org/free/fedora/rpmfusion-free-release-45.noarch.rpm
    2. AMD cards (Radeon): install the two “freeworld” drivers. Fedora's own versions are replaced by them (if they are installed, as on a desktop installation, dnf swaps them):AMD drivers
      $ sudo dnf swap mesa-va-drivers mesa-va-drivers-freeworld
$ sudo dnf swap mesa-vulkan-drivers mesa-vulkan-drivers-freeworld
      If a package is not installed yet, sudo dnf install with the same -freeworld name does the work. The warning that the package prints at installation names only mesa-va-drivers-freeworld, but the test machines had both.
    3. Intel cards: enable the second RPM Fusion repository and install the Intel media driver.Intel driver
      $ sudo dnf install https://mirrors.rpmfusion.org/nonfree/fedora/rpmfusion-nonfree-release-45.noarch.rpm
$ sudo dnf install intel-media-driver
      If dnf says that it must replace libva-intel-media-driver, add --allowerasing to the second command. On a machine with both an Intel and an AMD card, do both steps.
    4. NVIDIA cards: the proprietary driver of NVIDIA. REMOTIX uses the card through Vulkan Video. It worked on a real NVIDIA card on Ubuntu, but it has not been run again on Fedora 45 (see 1.3 “What you need”).
    5. Check that the card encodes. REMOTIX runs this test for you at the end of its installation and warns you if it fails; the command to repeat it by hand is in 2.3 “Fedora 45: installing”.
    i
    What was tried. the encoding test succeeded on the Intel card of a real machine started from the Fedora 45 live system (25 November 2026), which also has a Radeon. A complete desktop on a machine with an Intel card alone was not tried.

    2.3 · Fedora 45: installing

    This section is for Fedora 45. The package is a single file named like remotix-1.2.0-1.fc45.x86_64.rpm (the last part is the architecture: x86_64 for an ordinary PC). Put it on the machine and, in the folder where it is, run:

    Install and start
    $ sudo dnf install ./remotix-1.2.0-1.fc45.x86_64.rpm
$ sudo systemctl enable --now remotix
$ systemctl status remotix

    The first command installs REMOTIX and what it needs. The second makes the service start with the machine and starts it now. The third shows that it is running (“active (running)”). The ./ before the file name matters: without it dnf looks for a package of that name in the repositories and does not find it. Use dnf install and not rpm -i: dnf reads from the package what REMOTIX needs and installs it from Fedora's repositories, while rpm fetches nothing and refuses the installation if something is missing. If you install the same version again over itself (for example a package rebuilt with the same number), use sudo dnf reinstall ./remotix-1.2.0-1.fc45.x86_64.rpm.

    What dnf brings along. Besides REMOTIX's own files, dnf installs what REMOTIX needs to run and the things it recommends:

    The video drivers are not in this table on purpose: they are not a dependency of the package, they are a requirement of yours (see 2.2 “Fedora 45: video drivers (RPM Fusion)”). A normal Fedora installation has most of the other packages already, so dnf often installs only a few of them.

    !
    Do not skip the recommended packages. if you install with --setopt=install_weak_deps=False you lose the recommended packages, among them, on XFCE and LXQt, labwc: the installation succeeds, but the desktop cannot be shown until it is installed. The package prints a warning when it sees this on a machine whose desktop is XFCE or LXQt, and the journal names the missing package (sudo dnf install labwc).

    What else the package puts in place. A SELinux module of its own, loaded at installation (the machine stays in enforcing mode), and the definition of a firewalld service called remotix, defined and not opened (see 2.5 “Fedora 45: the firewall”). If the module could not be loaded, the installation does not fail, but prints a warning that says so and how to load it.

    No desktop is installed by REMOTIX. If the machine has none of the four, the service starts but says in its journal that it has found no desktop.

    i
    Power off changes for everybody. from the moment REMOTIX is installed, nobody can power off, restart, suspend or hibernate the machine from a desktop's menu — not even a person sitting at the machine, and not from the login screen. An administrator still can, with sudo systemctl poweroff or sudo systemctl reboot (see 5.4 “Users, groups and what REMOTIX changes”).

    The video card test. At the end of the first installation REMOTIX asks the video card to encode one test frame. If the card does not, dnf does not fail, but prints a warning:

    A warning at installation
    remotix: ⚠ the video card of this machine did not encode a test frame (H.264): REMOTIX will not show any desktop until it can.
remotix:   on Fedora the H.264 driver is not in the base repositories: enable RPM Fusion (free) and install mesa-va-drivers-freeworld
remotix:   (NVIDIA: its proprietary driver), then: sudo /usr/libexec/remotix/remotix --encoding-test h264

    Once the driver is in place (see 2.2 “Fedora 45: video drivers (RPM Fusion)”), repeat the test yourself:

    Repeat the test
    $ sudo /usr/libexec/remotix/remotix --encoding-test h264

    It prints one line and ends with 0 if the card encodes, 3 if no card of the machine can encode, 1 if a card opens but produces nothing. A virtual machine that has only the emulated card gets this warning, because the card exists but cannot encode.

    2.5 · Fedora 45: the firewall

    REMOTIX never changes the firewall: the package only describes what has to be opened. On Fedora 45 the firewall is firewalld, and what has to be open is the port 7447, both TCP and UDP. With only TCP open the page loads and the desktop never arrives.

    Is it already open? It depends on the edition. On the test machines (a fresh installation, 21-22 November 2026) the default zone of Fedora Workstation (GNOME) and of the KDE Plasma edition leaves the ports from 1025 upward open for everybody, so 7447 was reachable without doing anything; on the Xfce and LXQt spins the zone was public and the port was closed. Do not rely on either: look at your own machine with sudo firewall-cmd --get-active-zones and sudo firewall-cmd --list-all, and open the port as shown below if you want it open on purpose (for example if you later narrow the zone).

    With firewalld, the package adds a service called remotix that stands for exactly those two ports. It is defined but not opened. To open it, for good, in the default zone:

    Open the port with firewalld
    $ sudo firewall-cmd --permanent --add-service=remotix && sudo firewall-cmd --reload
    • For another zone, add --zone=NAME to the first command.
    • To close it again: sudo firewall-cmd --permanent --remove-service=remotix && sudo firewall-cmd --reload.
    • The package makes the running firewall know the new service when it is installed, so the first command works right after the installation. Without --permanent the rule lasts only until the next reload or restart of the firewall.

    With another firewall, or a firewall outside the machine (a provider's, a router's), open 7447 for TCP and for UDP by hand.

    Letting only your own network in, instead of everybody, is done with a more specific rule or zone of firewalld; the command above does not do it, and no such rule was tried for this manual.

    i
    A different port. if you change the port (see 2.7 “Fedora 45: port, name and other settings”), the firewalld service still says 7447. Open the new port instead, for TCP and for UDP.

    2.6 · Fedora 45: the first visit and the certificate

    REMOTIX is reached with an address like https://machine-name:7447 (or the machine's IP address instead of its name; see below). Everything is encrypted, but the certificate that proves the machine's identity is made by the machine itself the first time the service starts: no certificate authority has signed it, and the machine has no way of getting one without a domain name.

    So, the first time you open the page on each device, the browser warns you. This is expected:

    1. Open the address in the browser.
    2. The browser shows a warning page (Chrome and Edge: “Your connection is not private”; Firefox: “Warning: Potential Security Risk Ahead”; the words vary with the browser and its language).
    3. Choose Advanced, then the link that continues to the site (Chrome and Edge: “Proceed to … (unsafe)”; Firefox: “Accept the Risk and Continue”).
    4. The REMOTIX sign-in page opens. The browser remembers your choice for this device.
    !
    Do it once, on your own network. that first click is a real trust decision: it is safe only if you are really talking to your machine. Do it from a network you trust (your own network or a VPN). Someone who sits between you and the machine could in principle answer instead of it the very first time; after that, the browser would notice a different certificate. See 5.5 “Security on a shared server”.

    The name in the certificate. The certificate carries the machine's host name (what the command hostname prints), and the browser expects to be reached with that name. If people connect with the IP address or with another name, the browser shows a different warning, and some browsers do not offer a way to continue. To make a certificate for another name or address, write it in the settings (--name, see 2.7 “Fedora 45: port, name and other settings”) before the service is started for the first time. The certificate is made once and then kept; to replace it later with a new self-made one, do this:

    Make the machine's certificate again
    $ sudo systemctl stop remotix
$ sudo mkdir -p /root/remotix-old-certificate
$ sudo sh -c 'mv /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/page.* /root/remotix-old-certificate/'
$ sudo systemctl start remotix

    The third command is written with sh -c and quotes on purpose: the folder can be read only by root and by REMOTIX, so the page.* has to be worked out by root. After this every browser asks once more. Stopping the service closes the desktops of the people who are working: warn them first.

    Your own certificate. If you have a domain name and a real certificate (from Let's Encrypt or from your organisation), REMOTIX will use it for the page, and the browser's warning disappears. Put the certificate (with its chain) and its key in the folder where REMOTIX keeps its own, under the names page.pem and page.key, owned by the user remotix. Start from the commands above (stop the service, move the old page.* files out of the way), then:

    Use a real certificate
    $ sudo cp fullchain.pem /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/page.pem
$ sudo cp privkey.pem /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/page.key
$ sudo chown remotix:remotix /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/page.pem /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/page.key
$ sudo chmod 600 /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/page.key
$ sudo systemctl start remotix

    The journal then says “the page uses a certificate that is not ours”. A certificate that REMOTIX did not make is used as it is and never regenerated, not even when it expires: renewing it is your job (copy the new files in the same way and restart the service). The address people type must be one of the names in the certificate. This was written from what the program does; it has not yet been tried in this release with a certificate from a real authority, nor with SELinux's labels of the copied files looked at (a file made by cp in that folder takes the folder's label, which is the right one).

    i
    A second certificate. the video and the keys travel over a second connection (QUIC, UDP port 7447) that uses a short-lived certificate REMOTIX makes and renews by itself; the page hands its fingerprint to the browser. You never see it and never have to touch it, even when you use your own certificate for the page.

    2.7 · Fedora 45: port, name and other settings

    The settings are a few lines in a small file. REMOTIX's defaults live in /usr/share/remotix/remotix.conf: do not edit that file, an upgrade rewrites it. Your choices go in a file of your own ending in .conf in /etc/remotix/remotix.conf.d/, with the same kind of lines; they win over the defaults. If several files set the same line, the last one in alphabetical order wins.

    Create the file
    $ sudo nano /etc/remotix/remotix.conf.d/local.conf

    A file that changes the port, names the machine by its IP address and lowers the ceiling to six sessions looks like this:

    /etc/remotix/remotix.conf.d/local.conf
    REMOTIX_PORT=7500
REMOTIX_OPTIONS=--name 192.168.1.10 --session-cap 6

    Then restart the service for the change to take effect:

    Apply
    $ sudo systemctl restart remotix

    The two lines are REMOTIX_PORT (the port, for TCP and UDP; default 7447) and REMOTIX_OPTIONS (extra options, separated by spaces). Which options are safe to use, and what they do, is in 5.3 “Settings you can change”. Restarting the service closes the people's desktops and the programs in them (see 5.2 “What a restart of the service does”): warn them first.

    i
    Changing the port. the page and the video use the same port number. If you change it, open the new port in the firewall for TCP and UDP (the firewalld service remotix says 7447 whatever you choose, so use sudo firewall-cmd --permanent --add-port=7500/tcp --add-port=7500/udp and then sudo firewall-cmd --reload), and tell people to type it after the name: https://machine-name:7500. SELinux's module of REMOTIX lets the service use any port from 1024 upward that has no label of its own, and the web ports (80, 443, 8080 and the like); a port that Fedora already labels for another service would need an extra SELinux command, as for any server. A different port was not tried with SELinux enforcing.

    2.8 · Fedora 45: who may connect

    Anyone who has a user account on the machine, with a password, can sign in with that name and password. The password is checked by the machine's own authentication (the same stack that ssh uses, defined in /etc/pam.d/remotix), so whatever the machine already enforces — a locked or expired account, a nologin file, a failure counter if you have set up one — holds for REMOTIX too. (A fresh Fedora Workstation has no failure counter such as pam_faillock switched on, for ssh as for REMOTIX: see 5.5 “Security on a shared server”.)

    Who is excluded. The file /etc/remotix/denied-users lists, one per line, the user names that can never sign in through REMOTIX, whatever their password. It comes with one line, root. To exclude someone else:

    Exclude a user
    $ echo anna | sudo tee -a /etc/remotix/denied-users
$ cat /etc/remotix/denied-users
    • The change holds from the next sign-in; it does not disconnect someone who is already working.
    • Keep root in the file. The file must stay owned by root and not writable by others, and must exist: if it is missing or damaged, nobody can sign in.
    • To close an account to REMOTIX and everything else, lock the account in the usual way for the machine (for example sudo passwd -l anna).

    Two more things stop a sign-in, and they are not settings: a user who is already signed in at the machine itself, in front of its screen, is refused remotely; and a user who is already connected from another browser is refused a second connection (see ch. 4 “When something does not work”). And the other way round: if a user signs in at the machine's own screen while their remote desktop is open, that remote desktop is closed with its programs.

    2.9 · Fedora 45: upgrading and removing

    Upgrading. Install the newer file in the same way. If the service is running, it is restarted by the package; if it is switched off, it stays off. Warn the people connected first, as for a removal.

    Upgrade
    $ sudo dnf install ./remotix-NEW-VERSION.x86_64.rpm
    • The restart closes every remote desktop with its programs: unsaved work is lost. At the next sign-in people get a new desktop.
    • If you changed a file that the package also ships (for example /etc/pam.d/remotix), rpm keeps your file and, if the package's own version of it changed, puts the new one beside it with the ending .rpmnew; compare the two when you have time. The files concerned are /etc/pam.d/remotix, /etc/remotix/denied-users and the needrestart file.
    • An upgrade is never refused because of the video card.
    • If the machine has needrestart (Fedora does not install it by itself), the package tells it never to restart REMOTIX by itself, because a restart closes every desktop: the service is then listed as one to restart by hand, which you do when nobody is working. This was written from the package; it was not seen at work.

    Removing. There is one command. An RPM package has no separate “purge”: removing REMOTIX also deletes what it keeps, so that the machine is left clean. Warn the people connected first: it closes the remote desktops and everything running in them, and unsaved work is lost.

    Remove, and delete everything REMOTIX keeps
    $ sudo dnf remove remotix
    • The service is stopped and the remote desktops are closed (each is asked to end, and any that has not ended after fifteen seconds is killed: only the programs of that desktop, nothing else).
    • The users that REMOTIX had added to a group for the video card are taken out of it again, and the permissions it gave on the card are taken back.
    • The lines REMOTIX had added to the sign-in files of the login screens (in /etc/pam.d/: gdm, plasmalogin, lightdm and sddm) are taken out again, and the files of the login screen are as they were before (see 5.4 “Users, groups and what REMOTIX changes”).
    • The rules database for GNOME sessions, /etc/dconf/db/remotix, that the package made is deleted, and the SELinux module of REMOTIX is unloaded.
    • The two polkit rules, including the one that forbids power off, go away with the package: the desktops' power entries are the machine's own again.
    • The package's own data go: the certificates and the ban list (/var/lib/remotix/net), the record of group changes, the files REMOTIX keeps in each user's own folder — in ~/.local/state/remotix/ (the session log and a few small records; the folder too if nothing else is in it) and, on GNOME, the small file ~/.config/dconf/remotix (three keyboard values) — and the system user remotix, unless it was already on the machine before REMOTIX. Only these files are removed, and as that user: the rest of the user's folders is never touched.
    • What stays: your own files in /etc/remotix/remotix.conf.d/ (and the folder that holds them), and what you did yourself: a firewall rule you added (for example firewall-cmd --add-service=remotix) is not removed by REMOTIX; take it out yourself if you do not want it. A settings file of the package that you had modified (for example /etc/remotix/denied-users) is renamed by rpm with the ending .rpmsave and then deleted too: keep a copy of it if you want it.
    • Your own documents and the users' desktop settings are never touched.

    What the removal does not reach. It acts on the users the system lists at that moment. A user who is not listed then (a network home folder that is down, an account in a directory that cannot be reached) keeps those files: they do nothing without REMOTIX, and you can delete them by hand. A file of your own that you put in ~/.local/state/remotix/ stays, and so does the folder that holds it. The folder ~/.config/dconf/ itself stays.

    The packages that dnf installed together with REMOTIX (labwc, wlr-randr and the like, which came as recommended packages) are removed by dnf together with REMOTIX if nothing else needs them: they are not files of REMOTIX, it is dnf that cleans up, and installing REMOTIX again brings them back. Read the list dnf shows before you agree. sudo dnf autoremove also lists, before doing anything, whatever else is no longer needed.

    i
    What was tried. the removal and the reinstallation were tried on the Fedora test machines (virtual machines): the removal leaves the login screens' files as they were byte for byte, also when /var/lib/remotix was left over, and installing from scratch again works. The package 1.2.0 itself was installed from nothing, updated from the previous build and removed on the KDE test machine (25 November 2026): nothing of REMOTIX was left, except the firewall rule added by hand. They were not tried on a real machine other than those.
    Chapter 3

    Connecting and using it

    3.1 · Signing in

    Open the machine's address in the browser: https://machine-name:7447 (use the port the administrator tells you if it is not 7447). The first time on a device the browser warns about the certificate: see 2.6 “Fedora 45: the first visit and the certificate”.

    The page shows a sign-in form with two fields, Username and Password, and a Connect button. Type the user name and password you have on that machine — the ones you would use at its own screen — and press Connect. Between the two fields and the button there is a Keyboard menu, which tells the machine what kind of keyboard you are typing on (Italian, French, German, English (US) and so on: 33 layouts). It already shows the layout that goes with the language of your browser, so most people leave it alone; if your keyboard is different, choose the right one before pressing Connect. When you choose, the page remembers it on that browser and that device, and proposes it again next time; the browser language is used only until you have chosen. To change keyboard later, sign out and sign in again. At the bottom the page says “Encrypted connection” and the name of the machine.

    • The answer takes about a second even when everything is right, and a refusal for a wrong user name or password takes about two and a half seconds. That is deliberate: it makes it impossible to tell, by timing, whether a user name exists.
    • A green line then says “Admitted, new session…” (or “resumed session…” if you are coming back), with the size of the desktop, and the desktop appears in place of the form.
    • The first sign-in after the machine has restarted, after the service was restarted, or after you logged out, is slower: the page says Connected. The desktop is on its way… and the desktop appears by itself, in a few seconds on most desktops and up to about thirty seconds on the first start. You do not have to do anything.
    • Who can sign in: any user of the machine except root and the users the administrator has excluded, with the machine's password. Who cannot: a user who is already signed in at the machine's own screen, and, while the first is connected, a second browser of the same user (see ch. 4 “When something does not work”).

    3.2 · The desktop in the browser

    Once signed in you have your own desktop — GNOME, KDE Plasma, XFCE or LXQt, whichever the machine uses — in the browser tab.

    i
    Passwords inside the desktop. an administrator action inside the desktop (installing a program, for example) asks for a password, as at the machine. On GNOME the password window of graphical programs was not verified: in the automatic test on Fedora 45, a program started with pkexec refused to start in a remote GNOME session, so no password window could appear. If it does not appear, do the same action with sudo in a terminal. A few requests that a desktop makes on its own at sign-in (refreshing the software catalogue and the firmware list; for administrators, controlling the network and adding a system-wide software source), and that would only confuse, are settled quietly (see 5.4 “Users, groups and what REMOTIX changes”).

    3.3 · The keyboard

    Typing. Letters, numbers and symbols travel as letters: what you type is what appears, whatever your keyboard layout, and accents and symbols that are keys of your layout (Shift and AltGr included) come out right. Keys that are not letters — Enter, Tab, Esc, the arrows, F1 to F12, Ctrl, Alt, Super — travel as positions on the keyboard, and so do letters when you hold Ctrl, Alt or Super (a shortcut such as Ctrl+C is a command, not text). The desktop's keyboard layout is set again at every sign-in, from the Keyboard menu of the sign-in form. On GNOME the layout the page sends is written for the session in a small file in your home folder (~/.config/dconf/remotix), which only REMOTIX uses.

    • If characters come out wrong (for example a y where you typed a z, or a symbol that is not the one printed on your key), or a shortcut seems to be on the wrong key, check the Keyboard menu of the sign-in form: it must be the layout of the keyboard you are really typing on. Sign out, sign in again with the right one.
    • If you use a virtual machine (for example QEMU) inside the remote desktop, choose in the Keyboard menu the layout of the system that runs in the virtual machine: such programs pass on the keys by position, and the guest system turns the positions into characters with its own layout.
    • A character that is on no key of the session's layout (an emoji, another alphabet) produces nothing; it never produces a different letter. The server notes it in its log.
    • Characters you compose with dead keys (an accent typed first, then the letter) or with an input method (the kind used for Chinese, Japanese or Korean) may not arrive: the page ignores a dead key by itself and a keystroke that is still being composed. Not yet verified case by case. Characters that have a key of their own on your layout (for example é on an Italian or French keyboard) arrive normally.

    Combinations that do not reach the desktop. The page itself keeps no key combination: every command it receives is sent to the desktop, and the page asks the browser not to act on it as well (so Ctrl+S or F5 should go to the desktop only; this was not measured key by key). The one exception is Ctrl+V, which the browser must see in order to paste. What cannot be recovered is what the browser or your own computer keeps before the page sees it: in a normal Chrome window, for example, Ctrl+W still closes the tab. The table comes from measurements made on Debian 13, on GNOME with Chrome 151 and Firefox 140 ESR; they were not repeated on Fedora, but what a browser keeps depends on the browser and on your own computer, not on the machine you connect to, so the table is the best guide there is:

    • Your own computer may keep others that depend on it and were not measured: the Super (Windows) key, Alt+Tab, and combinations of Ctrl+Alt with the arrows or the function keys are the usual ones. On Android and Samsung DeX every combination with the Super key is kept by Android.
    • The table is not complete for other browsers, other systems or Firefox in full screen: not yet verified there. Safari, macOS and iPhone were not tried at all.
    • The page offers no way to send the keys the browser keeps, and no on-screen Ctrl+Alt+Del button: it is not available in the page as it is shipped. Use the desktop's own menus (for example “Log Out…”) instead of key combinations; for windows, the mouse works wherever a shortcut does not.

    3.4 · Copy and paste

    Text only, in both directions. You can copy text on your device and paste it in the remote desktop, and copy in the remote desktop and paste on your device. Images, files and formatting do not travel; the limit is one million bytes per copy. The tab of REMOTIX has to be the one in front (the browser lets a page write to your clipboard only then).

    Safari was not tried.

    i
    What was tried. this is what the page is built to do. On Fedora 45 the automatic test of the clipboard (text, both directions) passed on XFCE and LXQt, and the owner of the project tried the clipboard by hand on GNOME and on KDE Plasma (21 and 22 November 2026) and found it working. Other browsers and Safari were not checked.

    3.5 · Leaving and coming back

    Your desktop belongs to you, not to the browser tab. Closing the tab, a dropped line or moving to another device does not close it. Stopping, restarting or upgrading the service, switching the machine off, or signing in at the machine's own screen with the same user does close it, with its programs: unsaved work is lost.

    !
    The one-hour clock. only your own input — keys, mouse, touch — counts as touching the desktop. A desktop left for an hour without any input is closed even if a video is playing in it.

    3.6 · On a phone or a tablet

    On a phone or a tablet, REMOTIX works like on a PC if you have a mouse: connect, sign in, use the desktop. If you only have your finger, the page switches by itself to a touch layout. You do not choose it: the page looks at what you use. A touch screen used with the finger gets the touch layout; as soon as you use a mouse or a pen it goes back to the normal one, and a finger touch brings the touch layout back.

    In the touch layout your finger does not point at the desktop directly: it works like the trackpad of a laptop. The pointer is drawn by the page and moves when you drag; a tap clicks where the pointer is, not where your finger lands.

    • What you type with the on-screen keyboard reaches the desktop as letters (corrections included); Enter and Delete as keys.
    • Rotating the device does not rotate the desktop: it shows bands and the picture is scaled to fit. To get a desktop of the new shape, close the tab and sign in again.
    • A phone held upright gives a tall, narrow desktop, which is odd for a desktop: a tablet, or a phone with a mouse and keyboard, is more comfortable.
    i
    Not yet verified gesture by gesture on a real phone. the touch layout has been exercised on a simulated phone, and the gestures were not tried one by one on a real phone on Fedora 45. What was tried by hand on Fedora 45 (the owner of the project, from an Android device, on GNOME, 21 November 2026): a 1440p video, which played very smoothly. On GNOME 51 the first pointer movement after signing in used to arrive with one coordinate at zero, so that a first tap could land on the edge of the screen: REMOTIX now has a software cure for it (the first movement is sent twice), which passed the automatic tests with one, two and three users and with the phone tests; should the first tap still land in the wrong place, touch again. (The cure can be taken out when GNOME corrects the fault itself.)

    3.7 · Samsung DeX

    Samsung DeX (a phone or tablet connected to a screen, with a mouse and keyboard) is used as a PC: the page sees a mouse and uses the normal layout. Chrome on Android is the browser. On Fedora 45 it was not tried (the owner of the project tried Samsung DeX only on another distribution); what follows comes from the design of the page.

    • No hover on the desktop. On Samsung devices Chrome does not report mouse movement while no button is pressed. Pointing, clicking and dragging land exactly where they should, but you do not see what needs hovering: buttons that light up, tooltips, the pointer changing shape at the edge of a window.
    • Combinations with the Super key never reach the desktop: Android keeps them.
    • Whether the browser can keep other combinations for the page on DeX is not yet verified.
    Chapter 4

    When something does not work

    4.1 · What the page says when you sign in

    These are the sentences the page shows when you sign in or when the server refuses you. They appear in red (green only for a normal end of the session) under the form. The page never says more than it knows, and on purpose it does not say why a password was refused.

    i
    Information lines. the page may also show, in a box below the form, a short information line: for example that your browser does not decode HEVC and H.264 is used instead (the image is there; it uses more bandwidth), or that “No frame has arrived in N seconds”, with the advice to wait: after a restart the desktop can take about thirty seconds to start. These lines are for information; they are not errors.

    4.2 · The login defences

    REMOTIX defends the sign-in against people guessing passwords, with three counters and a ban that grows. Only failed sign-ins count: a wrong password, or a user name that does not exist. Protocol errors, timeouts and the refusal of a second connection do not count.

    • The address is the whole IPv4 address, or the /64 network for IPv6. Behind a router that gives everyone the same address (a home network, a company), people share the address counter. As installed, REMOTIX listens on IPv4 addresses only, so the IPv6 counters do not come into play.
    • The ban grows each time it is earned again: 15 minutes, then 1 hour, then 12 hours (the last step is the longest, however many times it repeats). The memory of earlier bans is forgotten after 24 hours without a ban.
    • A correct sign-in clears only the failures of that user name from that address.
    • The ban survives a restart of the service and of the machine: it is kept in a file.
    • There is no unblock for the administrator. This is deliberate: no command, no file to edit, no button. A ban ends only when its time is over. The administrator can only tell the person to wait, or to use another address.

    While banned, the page still opens, and it says so in a red notice above the form (attempts exhausted) with the hours and minutes left, rounded up to the minute. The notice ends with The block ends by itself when that time is up: there is no other way back in.

    4.3 · The three clocks

    Three clocks watch a session. They are explained here because their effects are what people notice.

    The administrator can change the last two (see 5.3 “Settings you can change”). On Fedora 45 the tests of the life cycle (the checks called lifecycle and resilience) passed, but the values of those tests are not recorded here: that the 30 and 60 minute clocks were tried with their real values on Fedora is not verified.

    4.4 · How a session ends

    When a session ends or the connection is lost, the page returns to the sign-in form and explains.

    4.5 · Where the administrator looks

    The administrator has three places to look, three quick checks, and SELinux to keep in mind.

    1. The journal. REMOTIX writes to the systemd journal. Everything REMOTIX says, including the processes it starts for each user's desktop, carries the identifier remotix:

    The journal
    $ sudo journalctl -u remotix                     # the service, from its start
$ sudo journalctl -t remotix                     # every REMOTIX line, users' sessions included
$ sudo journalctl -t remotix -p warning -b       # only warnings and errors since the boot
$ sudo journalctl -t remotix -f                  # follow it live

    Lines about one user's session also carry the field REMOTIX_TENANT (the user name), and every line carries REMOTIX_AREA, the part of the program that wrote it. For example sudo journalctl -t remotix REMOTIX_TENANT=anna.

    At every start the service writes a report. Useful lines: “the desktop of this machine: …” (which desktop it found), “video.codec offered …” (the video card encodes) or “THIS SERVER CANNOT ENCODE VIDEO” (it does not), and the PAM service line (the sign-in file is there).

    SELinux. Fedora has SELinux in enforcing mode and REMOTIX brings its own module. Refusals appear in the journal as lines that contain avc::

    SELinux
    $ sudo journalctl -b -g 'avc:'                     # SELinux refusals since the boot
$ sudo semodule -l | grep remotix                 # is REMOTIX's module loaded?

    Use the journal rather than ausearch: on the test machines ausearch showed nothing even when the journal had the refusals. The line of the service's start-up report that says “SELinux: born in system_u:system_r:remotix_net_t:s0” shows that the network side runs inside its wall; if the module is not loaded the package says so at installation, and the service says it at start-up.

    The sign-in hook of the login screens. At every start the service checks that the line which makes a local sign-in close a remote desktop is in the login screens' files, puts it back if it is not, and writes one summary line (see it with sudo journalctl -u remotix).

    2. The user's own session log. Each desktop writes its own output to ~/.local/state/remotix/session.log in the user's home (if that folder cannot be used, to remotix-session.log in the user's runtime folder, which disappears with the session). Useful when the desktop is born but something in it does not start.

    3. Three quick checks:

    Quick checks
    $ systemctl status remotix                      # running?
$ sudo ss -ltnup | grep 7447                    # listening on TCP and UDP?
$ sudo /usr/libexec/remotix/remotix --encoding-test h264   # does the card encode? (exit 0 = yes)

    4.6 · What cannot be done, by design

    Some things REMOTIX does not offer, deliberately. They are not defects.

    • No power off, restart, suspend or hibernate from a remote desktop. The entries are not in the menus, and the machine refuses the request from any user, at the machine or remote. sudo systemctl poweroff as the administrator still works.
    • No unblocking of a ban by the administrator. A ban ends with time (see 4.2 “The login defences”).
    • No screen lock inside the remote desktop; the sign-in page is the only door.
    • No remote sign-in for root, and none for a user at the machine's own screen.
    • One session per user, one connected browser at a time.
    • No unlocked keyring in a remote session: a program that asks for it finds it locked and asks for the password on the remote screen (declared, see 1.4 “What REMOTIX is not”).
    • The page cannot send the key combinations that the browser keeps (see 3.3 “The keyboard”).
    • The keyboard is chosen at sign-in: to change it, sign out and sign in again with another choice in the Keyboard menu. Wrong characters or shortcuts on the wrong key almost always mean that the menu does not match your keyboard; with a virtual machine inside the desktop, choose the layout of the system in it.

    These are the problems known on Fedora 45 that a user or an administrator may meet. None of them stops REMOTIX from working, except the first, which is a requirement. What is not listed here was not seen, which is not the same as proved absent.

    Chapter 5

    Looking after an installation

    5.1 · The service

    REMOTIX is one systemd service called remotix. It is installed switched off and not started (see 2.3 “Fedora 45: installing”): Fedora's own rule is that a new service starts switched off.

    The service
    $ sudo systemctl enable --now remotix     # start now and at every boot
$ sudo systemctl disable --now remotix    # stop now and do not start at boot
$ sudo systemctl restart remotix          # restart: closes the desktops (see below)
$ systemctl status remotix                # is it running?
    • As installed it listens on all the machine's IPv4 addresses, on TCP and UDP 7447: the page and the sign-in over TCP, the video and the keys over UDP.
    • It is started as root, because it has to check passwords and create the users' desktops. The part that talks to the network runs as the unprivileged user remotix, which writes only in its own data folders (/var/lib/remotix/net and /run/remotix/net).
    • If the service stops by itself because of a fault it is started again after two seconds. If it cannot start because of a wrong setting (status 78), it is not retried: read the journal.
    • Stopping or restarting it closes the people's desktops: see 5.2 “What a restart of the service does”.

    5.2 · What a restart of the service does

    What a restart of the service does to people at work. Stopping, restarting or upgrading the service closes every remote desktop and its programs: each is asked to end, and whatever has not ended after fifteen seconds is forced. Unsaved work is lost, so warn people first. The page tells them the server is shutting down. When the service is running again, each person signs in and gets a new desktop.

    • If the service falls by itself (a fault) it is restarted after two seconds, and a desktop whose service fell is closed unless its user comes back within those few seconds.
    • Restarting the machine ends every desktop and every program in the same way.
    • A user who signs in at the machine's screen while their remote desktop is open waits a moment (a few seconds, at most about 25) while the remote desktop is closed; if the service is stopped there is no wait. In the tests on Fedora 45 (21 to 25 November 2026; GNOME on a virtual machine) the remote desktop was closed in about one or two seconds on GNOME, 1.7 seconds on XFCE and 0.7 seconds on LXQt. On KDE Plasma, if a program of the remote desktop does not close by itself, the wait is about five to six seconds (REMOTIX gives the KDE desktop 5 seconds, then closes it by force). These figures come from the tests, not from a promise.
    • If the machine has needrestart (Fedora does not install it by itself), the package tells it not to restart REMOTIX after a library update, and lists the service for a manual restart. Do it when nobody is working. This was read from the package: whether needrestart or an automatic updater really respects it was not verified on Fedora.

    5.3 · Settings you can change

    Settings go in /etc/remotix/remotix.conf.d/ as described in 2.7 “Fedora 45: port, name and other settings”. These are the ones meant for an administrator; put them on the REMOTIX_OPTIONS line (the port has its own line).

    !
    Leave the rest alone. only the settings in this table are meant to be changed. The program has many more options, used by the people who develop it; leaving them alone is part of what has been tested. A wrong option can stop the service from starting (the journal says which).

    5.4 · Users, groups and what REMOTIX changes

    REMOTIX adds very little to the machine. This is the whole list.

    Users' own settings. While a user's remote session lasts, REMOTIX applies the session's rules to the desktop (no shut down, restart, suspend or lock buttons; “Log Out” always shown) and takes them away again at the end. Everything else the user changes — look, themes, panels, the wallpaper on GNOME — is theirs and stays, also at the machine.

    On GNOME the rules are given to the session through the system database /etc/dconf/db/remotix described above, so that the user's own settings file is never written by REMOTIX. The only trace in a user's home is the small file ~/.config/dconf/remotix, which holds the keyboard layout of the session and which removing the package deletes. For this to work the dconf command (package dconf, recommended) must be installed. Without it GNOME still starts, but REMOTIX cannot lock anything: the session's rules are only suggestions that the user can change, and what the user changes from the browser is not kept at the next sign-in. The journal says so with a line that starts DECLARED FALLBACK and the installation prints a warning. To mend it, install dconf and restart the service (warn the people first). This case was read from the program and not tried on a machine. If the administrator has set a DCONF_PROFILE of their own, REMOTIX keeps it as the base of the session; that too is read from the program and not verified on Fedora.

    On XFCE the values of the keys REMOTIX locks are saved in ~/.local/state/remotix/ and restored when the session ends, or at the next start if it ended badly. On KDE Plasma and LXQt the rules live in the session's temporary folder and disappear with it. Everything else the user changes is theirs and stays.

    The keyring. REMOTIX does not unlock the user's keyring when it signs a user in remotely (the modules that do it at the machine's login screen are not in REMOTIX's sign-in rules). A program that asks for the keyring finds it locked and asks for the password on the remote screen. This is declared and was decided; it is the same on the other distributions REMOTIX runs on.

    5.5 · Security on a shared server

    REMOTIX is made for your own server, on your own network or behind a VPN. That is its stated assumption, and the notes below follow from it.

    Optional protections you can switch on. Two more protections ship switched off in /usr/share/remotix/belts/, because they change settings of the machine that are yours to decide. They stop the physical power, restart, suspend and lid keys of the machine (so that nobody at the keyboard takes everybody's sessions away with one key) and forbid sleeping at all. As installed, the machine's physical power button therefore still works (the polkit rule that stops the desktops' menus is the only protection in force). To switch them on (effective at the next boot):

    Switch the optional protections on
    $ sudo install -D -m 0644 /usr/share/remotix/belts/remotix-power-keys.conf /etc/systemd/logind.conf.d/remotix-power-keys.conf
$ sudo install -D -m 0644 /usr/share/remotix/belts/remotix-no-suspend.conf /etc/systemd/sleep.conf.d/remotix-no-suspend.conf

    They are not part of what was tested for this release; use them if the machine is a shared server that must never go to sleep.

    5.6 · Where things are

    Where REMOTIX keeps things on a Fedora 45 machine (the paths are those of the package's file list):

    Certificates. They live in /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/ (see 2.6 “Fedora 45: the first visit and the certificate”). The page's certificate lasts a year and is made again when it expires; the short-lived one used by the video connection is renewed by the service well before it runs out. The ban list is /var/lib/remotix/net/ban. It does not hold any user name (they are stored in a scrambled form), is rewritten by REMOTIX, and survives restarts; do not edit it.

    Chapter 6

    Glossary

    6.1 · Words used in this manual

    Abandonment
    The 60-minute clock: after an hour without any input from the user, the desktop and its programs are closed.
    authselect
    Fedora's tool that decides which sign-in rules the machine uses. REMOTIX never changes what it generates; the PAM service of REMOTIX only uses it.
    Ban
    The temporary refusal of sign-ins from an address, or of one user name from an address, after too many wrong passwords. It lasts 15 minutes, then 1 hour, then 12 hours, and only time ends it.
    Canvas
    The desktop's size in pixels, decided when you sign in from the size of your browser window.
    Desktop
    GNOME, KDE Plasma, XFCE or LXQt: the windows, panels and programs you work with.
    Detach
    To leave without ending: the connection goes, the desktop and its programs stay.
    dnf
    Fedora's program for installing and removing packages. REMOTIX is installed with sudo dnf install ./remotix-1.2.0-1.fc45.x86_64.rpm and removed with sudo dnf remove remotix.
    Encoding
    Turning the desktop picture into video. REMOTIX does it only on the video card, and on Fedora the driver that does it for H.264 comes from RPM Fusion.
    firewalld
    The firewall of Fedora. REMOTIX defines a service called remotix for it (port 7447, TCP and UDP); the administrator opens it.
    H.264, HEVC
    The two video formats REMOTIX can use. Your browser decodes them.
    Inactivity
    The 30-minute clock: after half an hour without input, the page is disconnected and asks for the password again; the programs stay.
    Log Out
    The entry in the desktop's own menu that ends the session and closes all its programs.
    PAM
    The machine's own system for checking passwords. REMOTIX uses it, through a service called remotix, so the password is the one you use at the machine.
    Port 7447
    The one network port REMOTIX uses, over TCP for the page and over UDP for video and keys.
    QUIC, WebTransport
    The way the video, the sound and the keys travel between the browser and the machine, over UDP. Your browser must support WebTransport.
    RPM, .rpm
    The kind of package file Fedora uses: remotix-1.2.0-1.fc45.x86_64.rpm.
    RPM Fusion
    A separate repository of Fedora software, where the video drivers with H.264 and HEVC are. REMOTIX needs them on the video card, and the administrator enables the repository.
    SELinux
    The security system of Fedora, on in enforcing mode. REMOTIX brings a module of its own that puts the program in three domains of its own, one of them a narrow wall round the part that faces the network.
    Session
    Your desktop on the machine, with its programs. It belongs to you, not to the browser tab.
    Slot
    A user's single place among the connected ones. Taken by the first connected browser.
    Touch layout
    How the page behaves when you use a finger and no mouse: trackpad-like gestures and a keyboard button.
    Wayland
    The kind of graphical session REMOTIX uses. Programs written for the older X11 still run in it.
