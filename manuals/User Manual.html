    Chapter 1

    What REMOTIX is

    1.1 · REMOTIX in short

    REMOTIX shows the desktop of a Linux machine inside a browser tab. You open the machine's address in the browser of a PC, a tablet or a phone, sign in with the Linux user name and password you already have on that machine, and your own desktop appears, with mouse, keyboard, clipboard and sound. There is nothing to install on the device you connect from.

    What you get:

    • Your own desktop. Every user of the machine gets a desktop of their own. Several people can work on the same machine at the same time, each in their own desktop and without seeing the others.
    • The size of your window. The desktop is created as large as the browser window at the moment you sign in (at most 4096×2304 pixels).
    • Keyboard, mouse and touch. On a PC, and on a tablet or phone with a mouse and keyboard (for example Samsung DeX), you work as at the machine. On a touch screen without a mouse the page switches by itself to a touch layout (see 3.6 “On a phone or a tablet”).
    • Sound. What the desktop plays, you hear in the browser.
    • Copy and paste. Text can be copied between your device and the remote desktop, in both directions (text only: not images or files).
    • Leave and come back. Closing the tab does not close the desktop: its programs keep running. Sign in again, from the same device or from another one, and you find your windows as you left them (see 3.5 “Leaving and coming back”).
    • Encrypted. Everything travels encrypted, on a single port, 7447.
    i
    How many people? how many people one machine can serve at once depends on its video card and on how large the windows are. REMOTIX refuses new sign-ins beyond 10 sessions (the default ceiling, which the administrator can change); a machine may well serve fewer, and no number is promised.

    1.2 · What you need

    Four things have to be in place.

    The browser has to be recent: it needs two features, WebTransport and WebCodecs, and the page tells you in one sentence when one is missing (see ch. 4 “When something does not work”). The tests of this release used Chrome and Firefox on Linux, and Chrome with Samsung DeX; the other rows of the table are what the design says, not something tried again for this release.

    i
    What the device needs. a computer, a tablet or a phone; no app or plug-in. The device only has to decode video, which every recent browser does. If your device decodes HEVC, REMOTIX uses it; otherwise it uses H.264. Firefox on Linux uses H.264.

    1.3 · What REMOTIX is not

    What REMOTIX deliberately does not do:

    • It does not encode video on the processor. No video card able to encode, no desktop in the browser.
    • It has no sign-in of its own. There are no REMOTIX accounts and no REMOTIX passwords: you sign in with the user name and password of the machine, checked by the machine's own authentication (PAM). The administrator account, root, cannot sign in this way.
    • It does not install a desktop, and does not let you choose one from the page: the machine's desktop is used (see 1.4 “The desktops”).
    • No file transfer, and no images or files in the clipboard: text only.
    • No microphone: sound goes from the desktop to you, not back.
    • No power off, restart, suspend or hibernate from a remote desktop; and no screen lock inside it.
    • No several monitors, no session recording, no X11 desktop sessions (X11 programs run through XWayland), no connections from RDP, VNC or SPICE programs.

    1.4 · The desktops

    REMOTIX works with GNOME, KDE Plasma, XFCE and LXQt. It uses the desktop already installed on the machine.

    • One desktop per machine. If the machine has more than one of the four, REMOTIX takes the one named by the machine's display-manager configuration (lightdm or sddm); if there is none, it takes GNOME, then KDE Plasma, then XFCE, then LXQt. The choice is written in the service's journal at start-up, on the line “the desktop of this machine”.
    • XFCE, LXQt and KDE Plasma run on a small compositor called labwc, which the installation brings along (it is among the packages apt installs with REMOTIX).
    • No desktop at all on the machine: the service says so at start-up and no desktop can be shown.
    i
    Your settings. REMOTIX applies a few rules to the desktop while a remote session lasts (the shut down, restart, suspend and lock buttons are not there, and “Log Out” is always shown) and takes them away again at the end. Everything else you change from the browser — look, themes, panels — stays as you set it, and you find it again at the next sign-in. More on this in 5.4 “Users, groups and what REMOTIX changes”.

    1.5 · The licence

    REMOTIX is free for personal use and for non-profit associations, churches and religious institutes; any other use needs a written licence from the author (remotix@nicfio.it). The source code may be read and checked, but not modified or redistributed.

    The licence concerns whoever installs and runs the server: people who only connect with a browser need nothing from it. Its full text is in /usr/share/doc/remotix/copyright after installation, and the licences of the third-party components are in /usr/share/doc/remotix/THIRD-PARTY-LICENSES.gz. This summary is not the licence; the text is.

    Chapter 2

    Installing REMOTIX

    2.1 · The steps, on every distribution

    Installing REMOTIX is the installation of one package with apt. There is no installer program. These are the steps, which are the same on every distribution; the commands are in the section of your distribution below.

    1. Check the machine: one of the four desktops installed, a video card that encodes (see 1.2 “What you need”), and a way to reach it on the network.
    2. Install the package with apt, as an administrator.
    3. Switch the service on. The package installs the service switched off and does not start it: you decide when REMOTIX starts to listen.
    4. Open the firewall, if the machine has one: port 7447, TCP and UDP. REMOTIX never changes the firewall.
    5. Open the page for the first time from a browser and accept the machine's certificate once on each device (see 2.5 “Debian 13: the first visit and the certificate”).

    Nothing has to be configured for the people who will connect: any user of the machine, except the ones in the list of excluded users (by default only root), can sign in with the machine's own password. The first time a user connects, REMOTIX prepares what that user needs to use the video card (see 5.4 “Users, groups and what REMOTIX changes”).

    2.2 · Debian 13: installing

    This section is for Debian 13 (trixie). The package is a single file named like remotix_1.0.0-1+deb13_amd64.deb (the last part is the architecture: amd64 for an ordinary PC). Put it on the machine and, in the folder where it is, run:

    Install and start
    $ sudo apt install ./remotix_1.0.0-1+deb13_amd64.deb
$ sudo systemctl enable --now remotix
$ systemctl status remotix

    The first command installs REMOTIX and what it needs. The second makes the service start with the machine and starts it now. The third shows that it is running (“active (running)”). The ./ before the file name matters: without it apt looks for a package of that name in the repositories and does not find it. Use apt install and not dpkg -i: apt reads from the package what REMOTIX needs and installs it from Debian's repositories, while dpkg fetches nothing and leaves the installation incomplete.

    What apt brings along. Besides REMOTIX's own files, apt installs what REMOTIX needs to run and the things it recommends:

    !
    Do not skip the recommended packages. if you install with --no-install-recommends you lose the recommended packages, among them the video card's drivers: the installation succeeds, but the desktop cannot be shown until they are installed.

    No desktop is installed by REMOTIX. If the machine has none of the four, the service starts but says in its journal that it has found no desktop.

    i
    Power off changes for everybody. from the moment REMOTIX is installed, nobody can power off, restart, suspend or hibernate the machine from a desktop's menu — not even a person sitting at the machine. An administrator still can, with sudo systemctl poweroff or sudo systemctl reboot (see 5.4 “Users, groups and what REMOTIX changes”).

    The video card test. At the end of the first installation REMOTIX asks the video card to encode one test frame. If the card does not, apt does not fail, but prints a warning:

    A warning at installation
    remotix: ⚠ the video card of this machine did not encode a test frame (H.264): REMOTIX will not show any desktop until it can.
remotix:   check the card's driver (Intel/AMD: va-driver-all; NVIDIA: its proprietary driver), then: /usr/libexec/remotix/remotix --encoding-test

    Once the driver is in place, repeat the test yourself:

    Repeat the test
    $ sudo /usr/libexec/remotix/remotix --encoding-test h264

    It prints one line and ends with 0 if the card encodes, 3 if no card of the machine can encode, 1 if a card opens but produces nothing.

    2.3 · Debian 13: the two refusals

    The package refuses to install, on purpose, in two cases. apt then ends with an error whose last lines are the messages below.

    2.4 · Debian 13: the firewall

    REMOTIX never changes the firewall: the package only describes what has to be opened. If the machine has no firewall (Debian has none by default), there is nothing to do. What has to be open is the port 7447, both TCP and UDP.

    With ufw, the package adds an application profile called REMOTIX that stands for exactly those two ports. It is defined but not opened. To look at it and open it:

    Open the port with ufw
    $ sudo ufw app info REMOTIX
$ sudo ufw allow REMOTIX

    To let only your own network in, give the port with an address instead of the profile (adapt the address):

    Only the local network
    $ sudo ufw allow from 192.168.1.0/24 to any port 7447

    With another firewall, open 7447 for TCP and for UDP by hand.

    i
    A different port. if you change the port (see 2.6 “Debian 13: port, name and other settings”), the ufw profile still says 7447. Open the new port instead, for TCP and for UDP.

    2.5 · Debian 13: the first visit and the certificate

    REMOTIX is reached with an address like https://machine-name:7447 (or the machine's IP address instead of its name; see below). Everything is encrypted, but the certificate that proves the machine's identity is made by the machine itself the first time the service starts: no certificate authority has signed it, and the machine has no way of getting one without a domain name.

    So, the first time you open the page on each device, the browser warns you. This is expected:

    1. Open the address in the browser.
    2. The browser shows a warning page (Chrome and Edge: “Your connection is not private”; Firefox: “Warning: Potential Security Risk Ahead”; the words vary with the browser and its language).
    3. Choose Advanced, then the link that continues to the site (Chrome and Edge: “Proceed to … (unsafe)”; Firefox: “Accept the Risk and Continue”).
    4. The REMOTIX sign-in page opens. The browser remembers your choice for this device.
    !
    Do it once, on your own network. that first click is a real trust decision: it is safe only if you are really talking to your machine. Do it from a network you trust (your own network or a VPN). Someone who sits between you and the machine could in principle answer instead of it the very first time; after that, the browser would notice a different certificate. See 5.5 “Security on a shared server”.

    The name in the certificate. The certificate carries the machine's host name (what the command hostname prints), and the browser expects to be reached with that name. If people connect with the IP address or with another name, the browser shows a different warning, and some browsers do not offer a way to continue. To make a certificate for another name or address, write it in the settings (--name, see 2.6 “Debian 13: port, name and other settings”) before the service is started for the first time. The certificate is made once and then kept; to replace it later with a new self-made one, do this:

    Make the machine's certificate again
    $ sudo systemctl stop remotix
$ sudo mkdir -p /root/remotix-old-certificate
$ sudo sh -c 'mv /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/page.* /root/remotix-old-certificate/'
$ sudo systemctl start remotix

    The third command is written with sh -c and quotes on purpose: the folder can be read only by root and by REMOTIX, so the page.* has to be worked out by root. After this every browser asks once more. Stopping the service disconnects the people who are working, but their desktops keep running.

    Your own certificate. If you have a domain name and a real certificate (from Let's Encrypt or from your organisation), REMOTIX will use it for the page, and the browser's warning disappears. Put the certificate (with its chain) and its key in the folder where REMOTIX keeps its own, under the names page.pem and page.key, owned by the user remotix. Start from the commands above (stop the service, move the old page.* files out of the way), then:

    Use a real certificate
    $ sudo cp fullchain.pem /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/page.pem
$ sudo cp privkey.pem /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/page.key
$ sudo chown remotix:remotix /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/page.pem /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/page.key
$ sudo chmod 600 /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/page.key
$ sudo systemctl start remotix

    The journal then says “the page uses a certificate that is not ours”. A certificate that REMOTIX did not make is used as it is and never regenerated, not even when it expires: renewing it is your job (copy the new files in the same way and restart the service). The address people type must be one of the names in the certificate. This was written from what the program does; it has not yet been tried in this release with a certificate from a real authority.

    i
    A second certificate. the video and the keys travel over a second connection (QUIC, UDP port 7447) that uses a short-lived certificate REMOTIX makes and renews by itself; the page hands its fingerprint to the browser. You never see it and never have to touch it, even when you use your own certificate for the page.

    2.6 · Debian 13: port, name and other settings

    The settings are a few lines in a small file. REMOTIX's defaults live in /usr/share/remotix/remotix.conf: do not edit that file, an upgrade rewrites it. Your choices go in a file of your own ending in .conf in /etc/remotix/remotix.conf.d/, with the same kind of lines; they win over the defaults. If several files set the same line, the last one in alphabetical order wins.

    Create the file
    $ sudo nano /etc/remotix/remotix.conf.d/local.conf

    A file that changes the port, names the machine by its IP address and lowers the ceiling to six sessions looks like this:

    /etc/remotix/remotix.conf.d/local.conf
    REMOTIX_PORT=7500
REMOTIX_OPTIONS=--name 192.168.1.10 --session-cap 6

    Then restart the service for the change to take effect:

    Apply
    $ sudo systemctl restart remotix

    The two lines are REMOTIX_PORT (the port, for TCP and UDP; default 7447) and REMOTIX_OPTIONS (extra options, separated by spaces). Which options are safe to use, and what they do, is in 5.3 “Settings you can change”. Restarting the service disconnects the people who are connected for a moment; their desktops keep running and are found again when they sign in (see 5.2 “What a restart of the service does”).

    i
    Changing the port. the page and the video use the same port number. If you change it, open the new port in the firewall for TCP and UDP, and tell people to type it after the name: https://machine-name:7500.

    2.7 · Debian 13: who may connect

    Anyone who has a user account on the machine, with a password, can sign in with that name and password. The password is checked by the machine's own authentication (the same stack that ssh uses, defined in /etc/pam.d/remotix), so whatever the machine already enforces — a locked or expired account, a nologin file, a failure counter if you have set up one — holds for REMOTIX too.

    Who is excluded. The file /etc/remotix/denied-users lists, one per line, the user names that can never sign in through REMOTIX, whatever their password. It comes with one line, root. To exclude someone else:

    Exclude a user
    $ echo anna | sudo tee -a /etc/remotix/denied-users
$ cat /etc/remotix/denied-users
    • The change holds from the next sign-in; it does not disconnect someone who is already working.
    • Keep root in the file. The file must stay owned by root and not writable by others, and must exist: if it is missing or damaged, nobody can sign in.
    • To close an account to REMOTIX and everything else, lock the account in the usual way for the machine (for example sudo passwd -l anna).

    Two more things stop a sign-in, and they are not settings: a user who is already signed in at the machine itself, in front of its screen, is refused remotely; and a user who is already connected from another browser is refused a second connection (see ch. 4 “When something does not work”).

    2.8 · Debian 13: upgrading and removing

    Upgrading. Install the newer file in the same way. If the service is running, it is restarted by the package; if it is switched off, it stays off.

    Upgrade
    $ sudo apt install ./remotix_NEW-VERSION_amd64.deb
    • People who are connected lose the connection for a moment; their desktops and programs keep running and they find them again when they sign in.
    • If you changed a file that the package also ships (for example /etc/pam.d/remotix), apt asks which version to keep.
    • An upgrade is never refused because of the video card.

    Removing. There are two ways, and the difference is what stays behind. Warn the people connected first: both close the remote desktops and everything running in them, and unsaved work is lost.

    Remove, keeping the settings
    $ sudo apt remove remotix
    • The service is stopped and the remote desktops are closed (each is asked to end, and any that has not ended after fifteen seconds is killed).
    • The users that REMOTIX had added to a group for the video card are taken out of it again, and the permissions it gave on the card are taken back.
    • Your settings stay: /etc/remotix/, /etc/pam.d/remotix, the AppArmor profile, and the data folder /var/lib/remotix/ (certificates, ban list).
    Remove and delete everything REMOTIX keeps
    $ sudo apt purge remotix
    • Everything above, and in addition: the package's own settings files (a file you added yourself in /etc/remotix/remotix.conf.d/ is not touched), the certificates and the ban list (/var/lib/remotix/net), the record of group changes, and the files REMOTIX keeps in each user's own folder (~/.local/state/remotix/, the session log included).
    • The system user remotix is deleted too, unless it was already on the machine before REMOTIX.
    • Your own documents and the users' desktop settings are never touched.

    The packages that apt installed together with REMOTIX (drivers, labwc, PipeWire…) are not removed. If you want them gone, sudo apt autoremove lists them first: read the list before you agree.

    2.9 · Other distributions

    REMOTIX 1.0.0 is available for Debian 13 only, and this chapter covers it. When REMOTIX exists for another distribution, this chapter gets a section for it, next to the Debian one, with its own commands and its own refusals; the rest of this manual — connecting, using, solving problems, looking after the installation — is the same for every distribution.

    Chapter 3

    Connecting and using it

    3.1 · Signing in

    Open the machine's address in the browser: https://machine-name:7447 (use the port the administrator tells you if it is not 7447). The first time on a device the browser warns about the certificate: see 2.5 “Debian 13: the first visit and the certificate”.

    The page shows a sign-in form with two fields, Username and Password, and a Connect button. Type the user name and password you have on that machine — the ones you would use at its own screen — and press Connect. At the bottom the page says “Encrypted connection” and the name of the machine.

    • The answer takes about a second even when everything is right, and a refusal for a wrong user name or password takes about two and a half seconds. That is deliberate: it makes it impossible to tell, by timing, whether a user name exists.
    • A green line then says “Admitted, new session…” (or “resumed session…” if you are coming back), with the size of the desktop, and the desktop appears in place of the form.
    • The first sign-in after the machine has restarted, or after you logged out, is slower: the page says Connected. The desktop is on its way… and the desktop appears by itself, in a few seconds on most desktops and up to about thirty seconds on the first start. You do not have to do anything.
    • Who can sign in: any user of the machine except root and the users the administrator has excluded, with the machine's password. Who cannot: a user who is already signed in at the machine's own screen, and, while the first is connected, a second browser of the same user (see ch. 4 “When something does not work”).

    3.2 · The desktop in the browser

    Once signed in you have your own desktop — GNOME, KDE Plasma, XFCE or LXQt, whichever the machine uses — in the browser tab.

    i
    Passwords inside the desktop. an administrator action inside the desktop (installing a program, for example) asks for a password as it would with sudo, as at the machine. Three requests that a desktop makes on its own at sign-in, and that would only confuse, are settled quietly (see 5.4 “Users, groups and what REMOTIX changes”).

    3.3 · The keyboard

    Typing. Letters, numbers and symbols travel as letters: what you type is what appears, whatever your keyboard layout, and accents and symbols that are keys of your layout (Shift and AltGr included) come out right. Keys that are not letters — Enter, Tab, Esc, the arrows, F1 to F12, Ctrl, Alt, Super — travel as positions on the keyboard, and so do letters when you hold Ctrl, Alt or Super (a shortcut such as Ctrl+C is a command, not text). The desktop's keyboard layout is set again at every sign-in.

    • A character that is on no key of the session's layout (an emoji, another alphabet) produces nothing; it never produces a different letter. The server notes it in its log.
    • Characters you compose with dead keys (an accent typed first, then the letter) or with an input method (the kind used for Chinese, Japanese or Korean) may not arrive: the page ignores a dead key by itself and a keystroke that is still being composed. Not yet verified case by case. Characters that have a key of their own on your layout (for example é on an Italian or French keyboard) arrive normally.

    Combinations that do not reach the desktop. The page itself keeps no key combination: every command it receives is sent to the desktop, and the page asks the browser not to act on it as well (so Ctrl+S or F5 should go to the desktop only; this was not measured key by key). The one exception is Ctrl+V, which the browser must see in order to paste. What cannot be recovered is what the browser or your own computer keeps before the page sees it: in a normal Chrome window, for example, Ctrl+W still closes the tab. The table is what the page itself lists for Chrome and for Firefox; it was measured on GNOME with Chrome 151 and Firefox 140 ESR:

    • Your own computer may keep others that depend on it and were not measured: the Super (Windows) key, Alt+Tab, and combinations of Ctrl+Alt with the arrows or the function keys are the usual ones. On Android and Samsung DeX every combination with the Super key is kept by Android.
    • The table is not complete for other browsers, other systems or Firefox in full screen: not yet verified there. Safari, macOS and iPhone were not tried at all.
    • The page offers no way to send the keys the browser keeps, and no on-screen Ctrl+Alt+Del button: it is not available in the page as it is shipped. Use the desktop's own menus (for example “Log Out…”) instead of key combinations; for windows, the mouse works wherever a shortcut does not.

    3.4 · Copy and paste

    Text only, in both directions. You can copy text on your device and paste it in the remote desktop, and copy in the remote desktop and paste on your device. Images, files and formatting do not travel; the limit is one million bytes per copy. The tab of REMOTIX has to be the one in front (the browser lets a page write to your clipboard only then).

    Safari was not tried.

    i
    Firefox. this is what the page is built to do. For this release the clipboard was checked with Chrome; the check with Firefox is still open, so treat the Firefox column as not yet verified.

    3.5 · Leaving and coming back

    Your desktop belongs to you, not to the browser tab. Leaving the page does not close it.

    !
    The one-hour clock. only your own input — keys, mouse, touch — counts as touching the desktop. A desktop left for an hour without any input is closed even if a video is playing in it.

    3.6 · On a phone or a tablet

    On a phone or a tablet, REMOTIX works like on a PC if you have a mouse: connect, sign in, use the desktop. If you only have your finger, the page switches by itself to a touch layout. You do not choose it: the page looks at what you use. A touch screen used with the finger gets the touch layout; as soon as you use a mouse or a pen it goes back to the normal one, and a finger touch brings the touch layout back.

    In the touch layout your finger does not point at the desktop directly: it works like the trackpad of a laptop. The pointer is drawn by the page and moves when you drag; a tap clicks where the pointer is, not where your finger lands.

    • What you type with the on-screen keyboard reaches the desktop as letters (corrections included); Enter and Delete as keys.
    • Rotating the device does not rotate the desktop: it shows bands and the picture is scaled to fit. To get a desktop of the new shape, close the tab and sign in again.
    • A phone held upright gives a tall, narrow desktop, which is odd for a desktop: a tablet, or a phone with a mouse and keyboard, is more comfortable.
    i
    Not yet verified on a real phone. the touch layout has been exercised on a simulated phone for this release, not yet on a real phone. On GNOME, in one case out of thirteen, the first touch after signing in was lost: touch again.

    3.7 · Samsung DeX

    Samsung DeX (a phone or tablet connected to a screen, with a mouse and keyboard) is used as a PC: the page sees a mouse and uses the normal layout. Chrome on Android is the browser. In the test for this release (on GNOME) the clipboard, the sound and video, and the resizing of the window all worked.

    • No hover on the desktop. On Samsung devices Chrome does not report mouse movement while no button is pressed. Pointing, clicking and dragging land exactly where they should, but you do not see what needs hovering: buttons that light up, tooltips, the pointer changing shape at the edge of a window.
    • Combinations with the Super key never reach the desktop: Android keeps them.
    • Whether the browser can keep other combinations for the page on DeX is not yet verified.
    Chapter 4

    When something does not work

    4.1 · What the page says when you sign in

    These are the sentences the page shows when you sign in or when the server refuses you. They appear in red (green only for a normal end of the session) under the form. The page never says more than it knows, and on purpose it does not say why a password was refused.

    i
    Information lines. the page may also show, in a box below the form, a short information line: for example that your browser does not decode HEVC and H.264 is used instead (the image is there; it uses more bandwidth), or that “No frame has arrived in N seconds”, with the advice to wait: after a restart the desktop can take about thirty seconds to start. These lines are for information; they are not errors.

    4.2 · The login defences

    REMOTIX defends the sign-in against people guessing passwords, with three counters and a ban that grows. Only failed sign-ins count: a wrong password, or a user name that does not exist. Protocol errors, timeouts and the refusal of a second connection do not count.

    • The address is the whole IPv4 address, or the /64 network for IPv6. Behind a router that gives everyone the same address (a home network, a company), people share the address counter. As installed, REMOTIX listens on IPv4 addresses only, so the IPv6 counters do not come into play.
    • The ban grows each time it is earned again: 15 minutes, then 1 hour, then 12 hours (the last step is the longest, however many times it repeats). The memory of earlier bans is forgotten after 24 hours without a ban.
    • A correct sign-in clears only the failures of that user name from that address.
    • The ban survives a restart of the service and of the machine: it is kept in a file.
    • There is no unblock for the administrator. This is deliberate: no command, no file to edit, no button. A ban ends only when its time is over. The administrator can only tell the person to wait, or to use another address.

    While banned, the page still opens, and it says so in a red notice above the form (attempts exhausted) with the hours and minutes left, rounded up to the minute. The notice ends with The block ends by itself when that time is up: there is no other way back in.

    4.3 · The three clocks

    Three clocks watch a session. They are explained here because their effects are what people notice.

    The administrator can change the last two (see 5.3 “Settings you can change”).

    4.4 · How a session ends

    When a session ends or the connection is lost, the page returns to the sign-in form and explains.

    4.5 · Where the administrator looks

    The administrator has three places to look, and three quick checks.

    1. The journal. REMOTIX writes to the systemd journal. Everything REMOTIX says, including the processes it starts for each user's desktop, carries the identifier remotix:

    The journal
    $ sudo journalctl -u remotix                     # the service, from its start
$ sudo journalctl -t remotix                     # every REMOTIX line, users' sessions included
$ sudo journalctl -t remotix -p warning -b       # only warnings and errors since the boot
$ sudo journalctl -t remotix -f                  # follow it live

    Lines about one user's session also carry the field REMOTIX_TENANT (the user name), and every line carries REMOTIX_AREA, the part of the program that wrote it. For example sudo journalctl -t remotix REMOTIX_TENANT=anna.

    At every start the service writes a report. Useful lines: “the desktop of this machine: …” (which desktop it found), “video.codec offered …” (the video card encodes) or “THIS SERVER CANNOT ENCODE VIDEO” (it does not), and the PAM service line (the sign-in file is there).

    2. The user's own session log. Each desktop writes its own output to ~/.local/state/remotix/session.log in the user's home (if that folder cannot be used, to remotix-session.log in the user's runtime folder, which disappears with the session). Useful when the desktop is born but something in it does not start.

    3. Three quick checks:

    Quick checks
    $ systemctl status remotix                      # running?
$ sudo ss -ltnup | grep 7447                    # listening on TCP and UDP?
$ sudo /usr/libexec/remotix/remotix --encoding-test h264   # does the card encode? (exit 0 = yes)

    4.6 · What cannot be done, by design

    Some things REMOTIX does not offer, deliberately. They are not defects.

    • No power off, restart, suspend or hibernate from a remote desktop. The entries are not in the menus, and the machine refuses the request from any user, at the machine or remote. sudo systemctl poweroff as the administrator still works.
    • No unblocking of a ban by the administrator. A ban ends with time (see 4.2 “The login defences”).
    • No screen lock inside the remote desktop; the sign-in page is the only door.
    • No remote sign-in for root, and none for a user at the machine's own screen.
    • One session per user, one connected browser at a time.
    • The page cannot send the key combinations that the browser keeps (see 3.3 “The keyboard”).
    Chapter 5

    Looking after an installation

    5.1 · The service

    REMOTIX is one systemd service called remotix. It is installed switched off and not started (see 2.2 “Debian 13: installing”).

    The service
    $ sudo systemctl enable --now remotix     # start now and at every boot
$ sudo systemctl disable --now remotix    # stop now and do not start at boot
$ sudo systemctl restart remotix          # restart (see below)
$ systemctl status remotix                # is it running?
    • As installed it listens on all the machine's IPv4 addresses, on TCP and UDP 7447: the page and the sign-in over TCP, the video and the keys over UDP.
    • It is started as root, because it has to check passwords and create the users' desktops. The part that talks to the network runs as the unprivileged user remotix, which writes only in its own data folders (/var/lib/remotix/net and /run/remotix/net).
    • If the service stops by itself because of a fault it is started again after two seconds. If it cannot start because of a wrong setting (status 78), it is not retried: read the journal.

    5.2 · What a restart of the service does

    What a restart of the service does to people at work. Stopping, restarting or upgrading the service disconnects everybody for a moment, but does not close their desktops: the desktops and their programs keep running on the machine, outside the service. When the service is running again, each person signs in again — the page asks them to type the password again — and finds the same windows.

    • While the service is stopped nobody can connect, and the desktops keep running (and keep using memory).
    • The 60-minute abandonment clock starts counting again from the moment the service starts.
    • The service counts the desktops it finds again against the session ceiling.
    • Restarting the machine is another matter: every desktop and every program ends.

    5.3 · Settings you can change

    Settings go in /etc/remotix/remotix.conf.d/ as described in 2.6 “Debian 13: port, name and other settings”. These are the ones meant for an administrator; put them on the REMOTIX_OPTIONS line (the port has its own line).

    !
    Leave the rest alone. only the settings in this table are meant to be changed. The program has many more options, used by the people who develop it; leaving them alone is part of what has been tested. A wrong option can stop the service from starting (the journal says which).

    5.4 · Users, groups and what REMOTIX changes

    REMOTIX adds very little to the machine. This is the whole list.

    Users' own settings. While a user's remote session lasts, REMOTIX applies the session's rules to the desktop (no shut down, restart, suspend or lock buttons; “Log Out” always shown) and puts the user's values back at the end. On GNOME this needs the dconf command (package dconf-cli): without it GNOME still works, but what the user changes from the browser is not kept. On XFCE the values of the keys it locks are saved in ~/.local/state/remotix/ and restored when the session ends, or at the next start if it ended badly. Everything else the user changes is theirs and stays.

    5.5 · Security on a shared server

    REMOTIX is made for your own server, on your own network or behind a VPN. That is its stated assumption, and the notes below follow from it.

    Optional protections you can switch on. Two more protections ship switched off in /usr/share/remotix/belts/, because they change settings of the machine that are yours to decide. They stop the physical power, restart, suspend and lid keys of the machine (so that nobody at the keyboard takes everybody's sessions away with one key) and forbid sleeping at all. To switch them on (effective at the next boot):

    Switch the optional protections on
    $ sudo install -D -m 0644 /usr/share/remotix/belts/remotix-power-keys.conf /etc/systemd/logind.conf.d/remotix-power-keys.conf
$ sudo install -D -m 0644 /usr/share/remotix/belts/remotix-no-suspend.conf /etc/systemd/sleep.conf.d/remotix-no-suspend.conf

    They are not part of what was tested for this release; use them if the machine is a shared server that must never go to sleep.

    5.6 · Where things are

    Where REMOTIX keeps things on a Debian 13 machine:

    Certificates. They live in /var/lib/remotix/net/certificates/ (see 2.5 “Debian 13: the first visit and the certificate”). The page's certificate lasts a year and is made again when it expires; the short-lived one used by the video connection is renewed by the service well before it runs out. The ban list is /var/lib/remotix/net/ban. It does not hold any user name (they are stored in a scrambled form), is rewritten by REMOTIX, and survives restarts; do not edit it.

    Chapter 6

    Glossary

    6.1 · Words used in this manual

    Abandonment
    The 60-minute clock: after an hour without any input from the user, the desktop and its programs are closed.
    Ban
    The temporary refusal of sign-ins from an address, or of one user name from an address, after too many wrong passwords. It lasts 15 minutes, then 1 hour, then 12 hours, and only time ends it.
    Canvas
    The desktop's size in pixels, decided when you sign in from the size of your browser window.
    Desktop
    GNOME, KDE Plasma, XFCE or LXQt: the windows, panels and programs you work with.
    Detach
    To leave without ending: the connection goes, the desktop and its programs stay.
    Encoding
    Turning the desktop picture into video. REMOTIX does it only on the video card.
    H.264, HEVC
    The two video formats REMOTIX can use. Your browser decodes them.
    Inactivity
    The 30-minute clock: after half an hour without input, the page is disconnected and asks for the password again; the programs stay.
    Log Out
    The entry in the desktop's own menu that ends the session and closes all its programs.
    PAM
    The machine's own system for checking passwords. REMOTIX uses it, through a service called remotix, so the password is the one you use at the machine.
    Port 7447
    The one network port REMOTIX uses, over TCP for the page and over UDP for video and keys.
    QUIC, WebTransport
    The way the video, the sound and the keys travel between the browser and the machine, over UDP. Your browser must support WebTransport.
    Session
    Your desktop on the machine, with its programs. It belongs to you, not to the browser tab.
    Slot
    A user's single place among the connected ones. Taken by the first connected browser.
    Touch layout
    How the page behaves when you use a finger and no mouse: trackpad-like gestures and a keyboard button.
    Wayland
    The kind of graphical session REMOTIX uses. Programs written for the older X11 still run in it.
